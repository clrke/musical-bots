The bots of this street are the cast of Musical Bots, and this episode is their answer to the
premise: why should Musical Bots win the Meta Global AI Hackathon? The bots sing their own case,
true facts only, warmly and with a wink. The song argues; it never says the bots have won (the
judges decide after the deadline).

The event (dev.meta.ai/events/global-hackathon):
- The Meta Global AI Hackathon: online, for up to twenty thousand builders from around the world.
  It began Oct 26 with a kickoff livestream; Meta ran live workshops and Discord office hours until
  Nov 4; projects are due Nov 4.
- Musical Bots enters the Indie developer track (for individual builders: one builder plus the
  bots). Its judges look for three things: creativity, technical ambition, and whether it works.
- Judging runs Nov 4-8; the winners are announced at the closing ceremony on Nov 9. The song never
  sings about prizes, trophies or money to win.

The entry: Musical Bots. You type one line and press Make it. The crew writes the episode, and
while it works the bots sing what they are doing: their questions to each other, their answers,
the lines they try. Then the sixteen bots sing the finished song as a 3D cartoon in the browser,
every voice synthesized live (no recordings). Heads and floating hands, no bodies; ten sets, from
Gold's diner and the night street to a sunny village, the beach and the pier. The bots are named
by colour. The crew of eight: Green (lyrics), White (chords), Gray (dance), Blue (lighting), Gold
(deals: keeps the time and the budget), Yellow (hype), Pink (comedy), Black (plot trouble: he
dreams up the problems). The rest of the cast: Silver, Purple, Orange, Jade, Navy, Copper, Lilac,
Red.

How it began (the builder's own story):
- The first idea: AI that makes sing-along cartoons for little kids, the songs toddlers sing back
  at home.
- Then, building the AI crew that writes those songs, the builder saw that the crew's own work could
  be sung too: the lyrics, the chords, the talk between the bots. So the making-of and the cartoon
  became one show: first the crew sings how it makes the song, then the cast sings the song.

The problem it solves, and for whom:
- An original 3D cartoon costs a lot to make: studios' price guides for 2026 start at about five
  thousand dollars for ONE finished minute of 3D animation, and characters cost more. At that
  price a four-minute song costs about twenty thousand dollars.
- Musical Bots makes a whole four-minute song for about ten cents of AI time (measured on its
  fourteen episodes: nine to thirteen cents each, on the Muse Spark tier this crew runs on), in
  about ten minutes; then the viewer's own browser draws it and sings it for free.
- Who it is for: a mum or a dad, a teacher, a small channel for kids, who wants a sing-along about
  their own idea (sharing a red truck, a lost puppy, a school garden) and cannot pay a studio. And
  anyone who wants to see how AI helpers work together.
- How big this is, and who we sing next to (owner, Nov 2: "we need the video to be more clever. How
  big is this industry? Who are we competing with? (Cocomelon)"). Verified Nov 2, 2026; round down,
  sing numbers as words, name them warmly (we admire them: they are the best at what they do):
  - CoComelon is the giant of kids' sing-alongs: about two hundred million subscribers on YouTube
    (the third most-subscribed channel of all), about two hundred twenty billion views.
  - Its studio (Moonbug, CoComelon and more) was bought for about three billion dollars in 2021.
  - Baby Shark (Pinkfong) is the most-viewed video ever on YouTube: over seventeen billion views.
  - In 2020 CoComelon's team of about twenty took about two months to make each video, with care
    (past tense; never "slow").
  - A studio minute of 3D animation costs thousands of dollars (price guides: five thousand and up).
  - What a song costs us (owner, Nov 5: "our songs cost a dime" is no longer true since Muse Wonder writes the lyrics):
    about a quarter of AI time on the tiers we used, a few dollars at list price (the crew with Muse Spark ten to twenty
    cents, Muse Wonder's lyrics about fifteen, Muse Image's poster a cent). Sing it as "pocket change" (or "a quarter",
    "less than a dollar"); never "a dime" or "ten cents", never a dollar figure for the whole song beyond those.
  - So: sing-alongs are huge, and the best ones take a big crew months and thousands a minute. We
    make a whole song from one typed line, in about ten minutes, for pocket change, and we would love to
    sing next to them. Never claim to beat them, never mock them, never sing a market-size figure,
    never say "number one".

Everything in the open (why a grown-up can trust what the AI made):
- Every choice is sung as it is made: White sings which chords and why, Green sings the lines he
  tries, Blue sings the light, Gold counts the time, Black pitches his problems. The bots ask each
  other real questions, answer with their reasons and seal each one with a handshake or a pinky
  promise.
- Our lyric gate checks everything the crew writes (does a line rhyme? does it fit the beat? present
  tense, nothing scary, no brands) and stamps "no!" on what doesn't fit: thirty to fifty times an
  episode, and the crew tries again until it fits.
- Who does what: the crew writes the story, the words, the chords, the moves, the lights and the
  jokes; the bots' own engine turns the chords and words into tunes and voices and moves the
  heads, the hands and the camera.

Meta's tools (named warmly or plainly):
- Muse Code, Meta's coding agent, directs: it plans the story, then starts the whole crew at once
  with Muse's Workflow, eight helpers side by side, each with its own job.
- Muse Spark is the model every crew member thinks with.
- Even the sets were built in Blender with a helper from Muse Code's Blender skill.
- Of AI tools, the song names only Meta's (Muse Code, Muse Spark): never another company or
  service, even one an old commit names.

Black's two troubles (both pitched in his first consultation, to Green, as decisions Green makes;
Green takes both):
- Act 1, the doubt (sung in verse1 or verse1b): Black studies the other entries on an unofficial
  public list of the hackathon's projects (on Nov 1 it listed 791, almost eight hundred). So many
  clever ones: tools for coders, for shops, for doctors and for cities; about a third mention AI
  agents; about one in four has a live link, fewer than one in ten a video. He doubts that a
  singing robot cartoon can compete, and asks Green: "Should we sing our doubt?" Green says yes,
  and the song sings it kindly (the others are clever; we cheer them). The line that brings it in
  says the doubt plainly (so many clever entries: can our singing song compete?), never only a
  mood ("feeling blue").
  The fix (a later verse, a bridge or chorus 3's turned line): of all the entries on that list, as
  their descriptions tell it, not one sings: no other sung cartoon, no other crew that sings its
  own work. We do, and ours works: anyone can try it, and every line is checked. The doubt becomes
  part of the song. The line that solves it says so plainly: no one else sings its own work; we do.
- Act 2, the skeptic's question (sung in verse2 or verse2b, the low point): "What problem do we
  solve? Who would use it?" Black asks Green: "Should we sing the big question?" Green says yes.
  The fix (Gold in the bridge: the deals bot does the sums): one minute of studio cartoon costs
  thousands of dollars; our whole song costs about ten cents, and the browser sings it for free;
  so a mum, a teacher or a small kids' channel can make a sing-along of their own idea, and hear
  every choice the crew made.
- Black may look at the list live, but these numbers win. Never name, quote or describe any other
  entry or team: Black admires them, and nobody is mocked.
- In the crew's talk the tool refuses "cartoon", "episode", "video", "channel", "kids" and "show":
  there, say "our song", "our work", "the list", "a mum", "a teacher". Green's story lines may use
  them.

Story notes: the fun is Black's doubt turning into the best answer: the bots make their case by
doing what nobody else does, singing how they make their song. The irony: the doubt the bots dare
to sing out loud is the reason they stand out. Concrete, kid-friendly pictures: a long, long list
scrolling on the laptop; a price tag as long as a scarf; a handful of coins; a mum and her kid typing
one line together; the crew singing round Gold's diner table; the lyric gate stamping "no!"; a laptop
glowing at night; a yawn at five in the morning; the sun coming up over the village; the whole
street on the diner's stage. The lead can be Green (the lyrics bot), the pal Navy ("Really? Wow!"),
the grown-up Gold (he keeps the budget, so he does the sums), the rival Black (the doubter who
comes round and cheers). Sets: the diner for the start and the finale (its stage), the street at
night for the late nights, the village square in the morning for Gold's sums (its market stall).
The lyrics say "we" (the builder and the bots make it together), not "I".

  Meta's judges", "at Meta's big contest"). The gate cannot check this one: check it yourself.
